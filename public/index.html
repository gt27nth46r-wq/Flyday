How a day (and each hour) gets rated

Each day card shows two grades - Day and Night - split at civil twilight (the same boundary the FAA uses to define "night" for logging purposes, roughly 25-30 minutes before sunrise and after sunset). Each grade is whichever rating - Good, Marginal, or Bad - shows up most often among that period's individual hours, not the single worst hour. A brief rough patch surrounded by mostly-Good hours won't by itself drag the whole period down; a tie between two ratings breaks toward the more cautious one.

Each hour's own rating comes from a checklist modeled on a standard GA "good flying weather" reference — surface wind, gust spread, crosswind, visibility, ceiling, dew point spread, pressure pattern, precipitation, TAF trend, and convection. Each item is Green, Yellow, or Red. An hour's overall rating isn't just "worst item wins" - a single stray Red on a soft signal like wind or gust spread, surrounded by otherwise-Green items, reads as Marginal rather than Bad. But a handful of items (ceiling, visibility, pressure pattern, precipitation, TAF-flagged hazards, icing, convection, and crosswind) are treated as critical: a Red on any one of those is a real go/no-go stop that good wind and pressure elsewhere can't outweigh. Multiple independent problems, even non-critical ones, still add up to Bad.

  • Good — most hours in that period are Good
  • Marginal — most hours have a caution flag or two, but nothing serious enough to call it Bad
  • Bad — most hours have a critical item at Red (ceiling, visibility, pressure pattern, precipitation, a TAF-flagged hazard, icing, convection, or crosswind beyond every runway), or multiple independent problems stacking up together
  • Unknown — no hours in that period had data yet (rare, usually day 7)

The "Full checklist" table on each day still shows a separate, detailed worst-case breakdown across daytime hours only (overnight conditions never affect it) - useful for seeing exactly *why* a day looks the way it does, alongside the at-a-glance Day/Night grades above.

Below the hour strip, a second row shows the official flight category straight from the TAF - VFR (green), MVFR (blue), IFR (red), and LIFR (pink), the same colors used on sectionals and every other flight-planning app. This is a different thing from Flyday's own Good/Marginal/Bad rating above it: the flight category is the TAF's own forecast, shown only where the TAF actually reaches (roughly the next 24-30 hours) - gray means the TAF doesn't cover that hour, not that conditions are fine. Each day also calls out its best time to fly: the longest stretch of daytime hours sharing whichever rating is best available that day.

Turbulence, icing, and convection are checked against real current AIRMET/SIGMET/convective SIGMET data for the airport's location, not guessed - but only for roughly the next 24 hours, since those products are short-fuse and can't meaningfully forecast day 5 of a 7-day outlook. Beyond that window (or if the check fails), those rows fall back to a coarser proxy or show N/A. Ceiling comes from the real METAR for right now, and from the NWS's own hourly forecast (the same data behind weather.gov's Hourly Weather Graph) for the rest of the week - a real forecaster-reviewed product, not a raw model guess. If a given hour has no ceiling entry at all, that's shown as N/A - it means the forecast didn't cover that detail, not that the sky is clear. This is "ceiling" in the proper sense too: the base of the lowest broken/overcast layer (or vertical visibility into an obscuration), not just any cloud base - a forecast of scattered clouds alone doesn't count as a ceiling. As with anything this far out, treat days 4-7 as good for picking a promising day, not for counting on a specific number - and cross-check against current METARs and the TAF as the day gets closer. A few rows — fronts (actual position), cloud type/vertical development, PIREP ride reports, and winds aloft — are still shown as N/A: there's no free real-time data source Flyday can reach for those, so it says so rather than guessing. Check those separately in a full preflight briefing.

Flyday is a planning aid built on the NWS 7-day gridpoint forecast (pressure, wind, ceiling, visibility, temperature, dew point, ice accumulation, and a nearby gridpoint used to estimate pressure gradient) and aviationweather.gov METAR/TAF (current conditions, near-term flight category). The gradient reading is a proxy, not a hand-analyzed surface chart. It is not a substitute for an official preflight weather briefing — always check current METARs, TAFs, AIRMETs/SIGMETs, PIREPs, and NOTAMs before you fly.
